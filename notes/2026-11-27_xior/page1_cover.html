Student housing: a shortage the market will not pay for

Demand is not the problem

Purpose-built student housing, residences let by the room or studio with services, is one of the few corners of European real estate where demand clearly exceeds supply. On the continent most students still live with their parents, in university halls or in small private flats; the share in professionally run beds is far below the UK level. Student numbers keep rising, international enrolment most of all, while new supply is held back by building costs, slow planning and expensive financing. Industry estimates put new deliveries at about 2% of the stock a year, short of growth in international students.

This shows in the operating numbers. In the better university cities buildings are close to full and rents have risen faster than inflation for several years: Xior, the only listed specialist on the continent, reported 98.5% occupancy at the end of September and like-for-like rental growth of 4.62%. Demand also holds up in downturns, when enrolment tends to rise.

The struggle is on the financing side

Strong operations have not protected valuations or share prices. Student housing is a long-lived, low-yielding asset financed partly with debt, so it is a bet on interest rates as much as on rents. When rates rose in 2022 and 2023, valuers raised the yields they apply to rents, values fell and leverage ratios rose mechanically. Listed owners have traded well below net asset value since, which makes new shares a costly way to fund growth. Growth now has to come from rents, disposals and costs, not acquisitions.

Politics is a second headwind

Student rents are politically visible. In the Netherlands, one of Xior's two home markets, the Affordable Rent Act of July 2024 extended a points-based rent cap to a much larger part of the private market, and heavier taxes on rental property have pushed small landlords out. That deepens the shortage but caps what owners can charge. The government has also sought to slow the intake of international students into English-taught courses. Rent caps, taxes and visa rules can each cut into the growth that drives the case, and they change faster than a building can be repositioned.

Financing costs are the other slow-moving pressure. Debt raised before 2022 was cheap and largely hedged; as hedges and loans roll off, it is refinanced at higher rates, absorbing part of the rental growth. Xior's average cost of debt was about 3.05% in the first quarter, 89% hedged for an average of 4.6 years: manageable today, a drag in the years ahead.

What this means for investors

The sector offers what few property segments can: full buildings, rents growing faster than inflation and a structural shortage. The price is a balance sheet sensitive to rates, and share prices that track bond yields more than rents. For listed exposure the debate is less about demand than about whether leverage can come down without selling assets cheaply or issuing shares far below asset value. Size is a further constraint: with about EUR 1.2bn of market value and EUR 1.4m traded a day, Xior is a small, thinly traded stock.
