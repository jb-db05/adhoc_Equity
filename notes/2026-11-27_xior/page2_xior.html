1. What the company does

Xior is a Belgian real estate investment trust, listed in Brussels since 2015, that owns and runs student housing in eight countries: Belgium, the Netherlands, Spain, Portugal, Poland, Germany, Denmark and Sweden, with about 22,000 lettable units. The EUR 939m purchase of Basecamp in 2022 took it into Germany and the Nordics, and also lifted its debt.

2. Why it could do well

Operations are at their best. Occupancy reached 98.5% at the end of September and like-for-like rents rose 4.62%; management expects at least 4.4% for the full year. Nine-month EPRA earnings, the recurring profit of a property company, rose 6% to EUR 74.0m. Guidance is EPRA earnings per share of EUR 2.30 for 2026 and EUR 2.40 for 2027, with dividends of EUR 1.84 and EUR 1.92 (80% payout). Property values have stabilised, up 0.95% since January on higher rents.

3. What sets it apart

It is the only listed pure play on continental student housing, with scale in fragmented markets, locations close to universities and its own operating platform. A small active pipeline of EUR 171m means growth now comes mainly from rents in existing buildings.

4. Cheap, or a value trap?

On paper the share is cheap: 34% below EPRA net tangible assets, 11.1 times 2026 guided earnings and a 7.2% dividend yield covered by recurring profit. The discount is the market's verdict on leverage. Loan-to-value has risen every quarter this year, from 49.87% at the end of 2025 to 50.56% in June and 51.31% in September, despite higher valuations, mainly because about EUR 80m of dividends is paid in cash. Bringing it below 50% by December implies disposals or other measures in the fourth quarter. Issuing shares at a 34% discount would destroy value, so asset sales are the realistic route, and their price against book value will show whether the valuations hold.

5. Main risks

Leverage above target, with rates staying high. Refinancing at higher cost as hedges expire. Rent regulation, mainly in the Netherlands (about a third of units), and curbs on international students. Disposals below book value. Size and liquidity: with a market value of about EUR 1.2bn and about EUR 1.4m of shares traded a day on average over three months, Xior is too small for many large funds, a sizeable order can move the price, and selling can take days in a stressed market. A change of finance chief during deleveraging: the CFO leaves at the end of 2026 and the successor, a former sell-side analyst, starts by 1 February 2027, subject to regulatory approval.

6. What to watch next

Loan-to-value at 31 December in the full-year results, expected in early February; the size and price of disposals; the cost of new debt; confirmation of 2027 guidance; the 2027/28 letting season.

CONCLUSION

Xior is running its buildings better than at any time since 2019, and its recurring earnings and dividend keep growing by about 4% a year. The share price does not reflect that: it reflects a loan-to-value above management's own ceiling that has risen through 2026. The yield and the discount to asset value leave room for a re-rating if leverage comes down through disposals at or near book value and long-term rates stop rising. Until that is visible, the balance sheet rather than the rental market is likely to drive the shares.
