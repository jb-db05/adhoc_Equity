Japanese trading houses enter a new phase

Japan's large trading houses (sogo shosha) have spent the past decade turning from commodity traders into diversified investment groups. Commodities still carry a large weight in profits, yet a growing share now comes from food, power, finance, mobility and consumer businesses that earn steadier returns through the cycle.

Capital discipline has become the common thread across the sector. Each of the large houses now runs a plan that links progressive dividends and share buybacks to a return on equity goal. Once investment needs are covered, buybacks have become the main tool to lift that return.

The commodity backdrop is mixed. Crude oil and iron ore prices could soften in 2027 and 2028 as new supply arrives. Copper stands apart: mine supply remains tight and demand from power grids, electrification and data centres keeps rising. Houses with growing copper volumes look best placed in this setting.

Marubeni's first-quarter results show this mix at work. Higher copper and coking coal prices and strong chemicals and gas trading lifted net profit by 21%, and management put the extra cash straight into a larger buyback.

Share prices have cooled since February. Marubeni trades 20% below its February high, even though consensus earnings expectations have risen by 14% since then. Its valuation multiple has come back from about 18 times expected earnings to 13 times.

What this means for investors

We see the trading houses as a way to own Japanese earnings growth with high cash returns attached. Within the group, Marubeni offers one of the clearest combinations of copper growth, a broad non-resource base and rising buybacks. The pull-back since February has made the valuation less demanding than in the spring, and the half-year results on 4 November could act as the next catalyst.
