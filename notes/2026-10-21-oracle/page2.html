4. Cheap, or a value trap?

On earnings, the share looks undemanding. At USD 131.14, Oracle trades at about 16 times its fiscal 2027 guidance for non-GAAP earnings per share (Syz calculation). On Bloomberg consensus forward earnings, the multiple is 16.1x, close to its 10-year median of 16.9x and well below the 20.6x median of the past five years (Chart 2), although growth is now much faster. EV/EBITDA is 10.3x.

On cash flow, it is not. Operating cash flow reached a record USD 23 billion in the quarter, helped by customer prepayments, but capital spending of about USD 28 billion left free cash flow at minus USD 5 billion. The forward free-cash-flow yield is -9.4%. The earnings multiple therefore prices profits that are not yet turning into cash for shareholders.

The value-trap risk is real if the build-out keeps absorbing more cash than it generates for longer than expected. The case for value is that, once capacity is built and contracts ramp up, a 33% operating margin and a 10.7% return on invested capital could translate into positive free cash flow.

5. Main risks

  • Financing. At 31 August 2026, Oracle had USD 125 billion of notes payable and other borrowings against USD 36 billion of cash, plus USD 288 billion of data-center leases due to start between late 2026 and fiscal 2029 and USD 34 billion of purchase commitments. The USD 20 billion share sale (about 141 million new shares) eased the balance sheet but diluted shareholders. S&P cut Oracle's credit rating to BBB-, one notch above speculative grade, in July.
  • Customer concentration. Press reports link around half of the backlog to OpenAI; Oracle does not disclose the split. A change in OpenAI's plans or funding would hit Oracle directly.
  • Backlog conversion. Management expects about half of the USD 664 billion backlog to become revenue over the next 36 months. Much of the newest contract volume would only affect revenue from fiscal 2028.
  • Execution. Project Jupiter in New Mexico has faced gas-pipeline and permit setbacks; Oracle has sent a force majeure notice to the campus owner and asked federal regulators to speed up approval, while saying the project remains on schedule.
  • Competition. AWS, Azure and Google keep broader AI tools and their own chips. Oracle could end up mainly as a lower-priced supplier of computing capacity.
  • Healthcare and security. Oracle is expanding in sensitive clinical-data environments while integrating Cerner. Its agreement with AstraZeneca, announced on 20 October, is non-binding and has no disclosed financial terms.

6. What to watch next

  • The second-quarter results: guidance is for revenue growth of 30% to 34% and cloud growth of 65% to 71% in USD.
  • Free cash flow and the timing of the peak in capital spending.
  • A broader customer base behind the backlog.
  • Capacity delivered on schedule, including the Project Jupiter permits and gas supply.
  • Further share or debt issuance, and the trend in lease commitments relative to revenue.
  • Operating margin as infrastructure takes a larger share of revenue.
Conclusion

Oracle has built a credible position in the AI economy, but it is no longer a defensive software compounder: it has become a high-growth, high-execution-risk infrastructure company that finances its expansion with debt, leases and new shares. For a risk-tolerant investor with a multi-year horizon, it offers a broader way to participate in AI infrastructure than a pure-play GPU cloud, thanks to its database, applications and multicloud reach. For a conservative investor, negative free cash flow, rising commitments and customer concentration argue against treating it as a low-risk core holding.

Our stance would become more constructive if free cash flow starts to improve, capacity arrives on time and the backlog becomes more diversified. It would become more negative if capital spending stays high beyond the expected peak, lease obligations grow faster than revenue or an anchor customer reduces its commitments.
