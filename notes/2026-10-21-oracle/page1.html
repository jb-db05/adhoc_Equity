AI infrastructure: the market now asks who pays

Over the past year, the market's question on AI infrastructure has changed. In 2025, investors rewarded almost any company able to show contracted AI demand. In 2026, the focus has moved to who pays for the build-out, how quickly new capacity can be powered and switched on, and what return it would earn once it is running.

Oracle sits at the centre of this debate. Among the large software groups, it is the one that has gone furthest in turning itself into a provider of AI computing capacity, and it is doing so with its balance sheet rather than with the cash surpluses that the largest cloud platforms enjoy. Its share price peaked at USD 328 in September 2025, after the company disclosed a sharp jump in its contracted backlog, and has since lost about 60% of its value.

Demand is not the issue. Oracle reports that demand for AI training and inference capacity continues to grow faster than supply, and its backlog of contracted revenue reached USD 664 billion at the end of August 2026.

The issue is the cost of meeting that demand. In a single quarter, Oracle spent about USD 28 billion on infrastructure, sold USD 20 billion of new shares, and it has a further USD 288 billion of data-center leases that have not yet started.

Power is the other bottleneck. Data centers need grid connections, gas supply and permits, and delays are largely outside a company's control, as the setbacks at Oracle's Project Jupiter campus in New Mexico show.

What this means for investors

AI infrastructure is no longer a theme where every new contract is good news. The companies that come out ahead would be those that turn backlog into revenue on time and fund it without straining their balance sheet. For Oracle, the next 12 to 18 months of cash flow and capacity delivery matter more than the size of the backlog. We are constructive on the business trajectory, but cautious on the risk-reward of the share.
