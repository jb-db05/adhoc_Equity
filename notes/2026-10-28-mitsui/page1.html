Japan's trading houses: from deep value to a cash-return story

What a trading house is today

Japan's five large trading houses (Mitsubishi Corporation, Mitsui & Co., Itochu, Sumitomo Corporation and Marubeni) started out as importers and exporters. Today they work more like diversified investment groups. They own stakes in mines, LNG and oil projects, power and infrastructure, chemicals, food, retail, healthcare and new technologies, and they trade around these assets.

Their earnings combine two parts. Resource businesses swing with iron ore, coal, copper, oil and gas prices. Non-resource businesses are steadier but grow more slowly. The share of each in profit largely explains how sensitive each house is to the commodity cycle.

Why investors looked again

Two forces changed how the market sees the sector. Since 2020, Berkshire Hathaway has built stakes in all five houses. At the end of 2025 it held 10.4% of Mitsui, according to press reports. In September 2026, press reports said Berkshire was considering raising these stakes. At the same time, the Tokyo Stock Exchange has pushed listed companies to improve capital efficiency and returns to shareholders. The trading houses responded with higher dividends, regular buybacks and return targets set against cash flow.

Where Mitsui stands

Mitsui is one of the most resource-weighted houses. In the year to March 2026, its Mineral & Metal Resources and Energy segments together earned about half of group profit (Syz calculation from company figures). Iron ore, mainly from Australia, is its largest single earnings driver. It also has large positions in LNG, copper and a stake in Vale.

Profit has eased from the record of the year to March 2023 as commodity prices normalised. In May 2026, Mitsui set out a new medium-term plan to March 2029. It targets profit of JPY 1.1 trillion, core operating cash flow of JPY 1.2 trillion and a return on equity of 12%. It also set a dividend floor and committed to return around half of cumulative core operating cash flow to shareholders.

WHAT THIS MEANS FOR INVESTORS

The sector is no longer the overlooked, below-book-value story of 2020. Shares in Mitsui now reflect a more visible return policy and a strong anchor shareholder. The earnings behind those returns still depend to a large extent on commodity prices and the yen. Anyone looking at the stock should weigh both parts.
