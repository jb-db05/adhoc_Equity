What the Meta glasses are

Ray-Ban Meta (since September 2023) and Oakley Meta (since June 2025) look like ordinary glasses but contain a camera, microphones, speakers and the Meta AI assistant. The wearer can take photos and videos, make calls, play music and ask the assistant about what they see. EssilorLuxottica designs, makes and sells the frames; Meta provides the technology, the software and the AI.

At Meta's Connect event in late September the companies added a camera-free model, Ray-Ban Meta Audio (from USD 349), a third generation of Ray-Ban Meta and new styles. Meta's AI agent, Muse, is due on the glasses, first in the United States.

Why they matter for the group

Size. More than 7 million pairs sold in 2025 made smart glasses the first new eyewear category in decades to reach that scale so fast. Press reports put wearables at close to 6.5% of third-quarter revenue; the company gives the growth rate (+20%) but not the amount.

Reach. People wear glasses all day, so they could become the everyday device for AI assistants, as phones were for apps. The group brings what technology companies lack: frame design, prescription lenses, brands and a global store network.

Position. Industry trackers put Meta's share of smart-glasses shipments between about 70% and 85%, depending on the definition, and EssilorLuxottica is its eyewear partner. Google, Samsung and Apple are reported to be investing in their own AI glasses, so the lead could be tested.

Dependence. The success is shared with a partner that is also a shareholder, and a growing part of the group's growth now depends on one partner's roadmap.

The privacy question

Camera glasses raise a problem that phones do not: the person being filmed may not know.

Recording. Meta says an LED shows when the camera is recording. A German test found the light too faint to see in daylight, and reports say it does not come on when the camera-based AI features are used, so people nearby may have no signal.

Data. Photos, voice and what the camera sees can be processed by Meta's AI. Regulators ask where the images go and how long they are kept. In Europe, images of identifiable people are personal data.

Faces. Meta has not launched a consumer facial-recognition feature, but press reports say it has considered one for future models, and civil-liberty groups oppose the idea. Researchers have shown that footage from the glasses can be passed through outside tools to identify strangers.

Reactions in Europe. In early October the Dutch chain Hans Anders stopped selling the glasses in the Netherlands and Belgium, and Wehkamp paused sales. The Dutch data protection authority warned against sharing videos of identifiable people without permission, a consumer group threatened legal action and a German organisation filed a criminal complaint. Calls for bans have been heard in several countries, but reports say the Dutch government is not rushing to one.

Why it matters for the investment case

The data is Meta's, but the brand on the frame is Ray-Ban's, so the reputational risk reaches the group. A retailer pulling the product shows how fast sentiment can turn, and new rules could force design changes that add cost. If wearing camera glasses becomes socially awkward, as with Google Glass a decade ago, adoption could slow. Camera-free models show the group can sell connected glasses without the issue.

WHAT THIS MEANS FOR INVESTORS

Meta glasses help explain the 40 times earnings the shares were valued at a year ago, and part of the fall since. The category is growing fast and the group is its main manufacturer and distributor, but the line carries three exposures: dependence on one partner, competition from larger technology groups, and public and regulatory acceptance. At roughly 6.5% of sales the group could absorb a setback; a valuation built on their growth could be more sensitive.
