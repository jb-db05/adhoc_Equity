1. What the company does

SpaceX operates three segments. Space builds and launches the Falcon and Starship rockets and the Dragon capsule. Connectivity runs Starlink, which had 13.6 million consumer subscribers at end-September, plus aviation, maritime and government services. Super Intelligence rents AI computing capacity (1.7 GW of nameplate compute at end-September) and sells software, including Cursor, acquired in an all-stock deal agreed in June.

2. Why it could do well

AI revenue is growing very fast: USD 7.6 bn in the third quarter, up from USD 0.8 bn a year earlier. Management aims for USD 100 bn of annual recurring revenue by December and guides first-quarter 2027 revenue at USD 33 to 37 bn. Starlink keeps adding about 1.6 million subscribers a quarter, and the planned purchase of Grain's 800 MHz spectrum would support a direct-to-phone mobile service. Full Starship reuse would lower the cost of every satellite SpaceX launches for itself.

3. What sets it apart

No other company combines its own heavy-lift rockets, the largest satellite network and the demand to fill both. Competitors have slipped: ULA's Vulcan failed on 15 November, ten minutes into its return to flight, losing 40 Amazon Leo satellites, and Blue Origin's New Glenn exploded during a pad test in May. Both setbacks delay Amazon, Starlink's main challenger. The upper-stage catch widens a lead that was already large.

4. Is the share cheap?

Not on conventional measures. At about USD 1.71 tn, SpaceX trades at roughly 30 times its annualised third-quarter revenue and about 100 times annualised third-quarter earnings. On the midpoint of first-quarter 2027 guidance, the revenue multiple falls to about 12 times, which shows how much of the case rests on the AI ramp happening on schedule. The share has swung between USD 108 and 202 since listing and now sits barely above its USD 135 IPO price, even though quarterly revenue has nearly doubled. It slipped 1.3% on the first session after the catch: the market is discounting the spending and the coming share supply.

5. Main risks

Cash burn: nine-month capex of USD 48.1 bn against USD 13.1 bn of operating cash flow, and the Apollo lease adds fixed payments from 2027. The compute targets depend on power and cooling being built in time. Press reports point to a lock-up expiry around 8 to 9 December that could release a large block of shares. Elon Musk controls more than half the votes, and both boards are studying a merger with Tesla. Starlink's average revenue per user is lower than a year ago. In Ukraine, Starshield speed restrictions, which SpaceX says have not changed, highlight the political exposure of a key government supplier.

6. What to watch next

The share's behaviour around the December lock-up; whether the USD 100 bn recurring revenue target is met; the fourth-quarter results and 2027 capex plan; the FCC review of the Grain spectrum (oppositions due 8 December); any terms from the Tesla committees; and the first reflight of a caught upper stage.

CONCLUSION

The Starship catch strengthens the long-term case, and the third quarter shows the AI business is real, profitable at the EBITDA level and growing fast. At around USD 1.71 tn, though, the share already prices much of that ramp while the company spends several times what it earns in cash. For investors who want exposure, building a position in steps, for example around the December lock-up and the fourth-quarter results, looks more sensible than buying in one go. The position size should reflect a concentrated bet on one founder and one very large spending programme.
