Canadian oil sands: record cash, borrowed in part from geopolitics

An oil price set by events, not by demand

Brent has moved from close to USD 60 a barrel in mid-December 2025 to around USD 110 to 115 at the end of October 2026, after peaking near USD 138 in early April. The driver is supply risk in the Middle East, including disruption to shipping through the Strait of Hormuz, rather than a surge in global demand. Prices set by a conflict can fall quickly when tensions ease.

Oil sands producers are among the most exposed to this move. Their costs are largely fixed, so most of each extra dollar per barrel flows straight to cash flow. Cenovus generated CAD 5.0bn of adjusted funds flow in the second quarter of 2026, against CAD 3.4bn in the first quarter.

Consolidation and better market access

The sector has moved from growth at any cost to scale and returns. Cenovus closed its purchase of MEG Energy in November 2025 for more than CAD 8.6bn including assumed debt, and on 5 October 2026 agreed to buy Athabasca Oil for an implied enterprise value of CAD 5.7bn. Buying leases next to existing plants lets the buyer share facilities and cut unit costs.

The Trans Mountain expansion, in service since May 2024, gives Canadian heavy crude a route to Pacific markets and has reduced the discount at which it trades to US benchmarks.

WHAT THIS MEANS FOR INVESTORS

Today's cash flows are exceptional and largely returned to shareholders. The question for an investor is how much of the share price now depends on oil staying above USD 100. For Cenovus, the operating progress is real, but the low earnings multiple mostly reflects earnings the market expects to fade.
