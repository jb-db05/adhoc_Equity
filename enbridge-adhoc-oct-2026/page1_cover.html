North American pipelines: steady cash, harder growth

A sector valued for its income

Large pipeline operators earn most of their profits from regulated tariffs and long-term contracts that pay for reserved capacity whatever the volumes or the oil price. Investors therefore treat them partly as bond substitutes: they own them for a high, growing dividend rather than for rapid earnings growth.

Demand is supportive. Gas exports as liquefied natural gas are expanding, data centres need new gas-fired power, and Canadian crude still depends on pipelines to reach US refineries. Assets already in the ground gain value because new routes are hard to permit.

Growth now comes through deals and debt

2026 has seen a wave of pipeline acquisitions. Enbridge agreed to buy Tallgrass's crude business for USD 2.55bn and Salt Creek Midstream's crude gathering business for USD 600m, and Williams bought Momentum Midstream for USD 5.5bn. These deals add earnings, but they are paid for with debt and new shares, so growth per share is slower than growth in total profits.

WHAT THIS MEANS FOR INVESTORS

The sector suits investors seeking dependable income. The key checks are what is paid for that income, and whether the dividend can keep growing without stretching the balance sheet. For Enbridge, the dividend is secure, but per-share growth has stalled while the valuation has risen.
